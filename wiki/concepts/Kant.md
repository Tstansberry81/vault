# Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

**German philosopher**; one of the central figures of the Enlightenment and the most influential philosopher of the modern era. **Kant is the subject of [[Traveler Stansberry]]'s Fall 2026 philosophy coursework, specifically his ethical theory and the Categorical Imperative.**

## Quick Reference

- **Era:** Enlightenment (late 18th century)
- **Key works:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (epistemology/metaphysics), *Critique of Practical Reason* (ethics), *Grounding for the Metaphysics of Morals* (ethics) — **the primary text for [[PHIL 1730 (Kant's Deontology, UVA Fall 2026)|PHIL 1730]]**
- **Core idea:** Knowledge, morality, and aesthetics are shaped by the structure of the human mind and rational agency; synthesis of rationalism and empiricism
- **Ethics:** Deontological (duty-based); the Categorical Imperative as the supreme moral principle
- **Current study:** Traveler is reading *Grounding for the Metaphysics of Morals*, Sections I–III (Due Tue 9/22 for Sections II & III; first exam Tue 9/29)

## Core Ideas

### Deontological Ethics (Moral Philosophy)

Kant's ethical theory is **deontological** (duty-focused, not consequence-focused). Key principles:

1. **Categorical Imperative (CI):** The supreme principle of morality. Acts are right iff they could be universal laws. Often formulated as "act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
   - **First formulation:** Universal Law formulation (above)
   - **Second formulation:** Humanity formulation — "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."
   - **Third formulation:** Kingdom of Ends formulation (synthetic principle)

2. **Autonomy:** Moral agents are rational beings with the capacity to give themselves moral law. Autonomy is the source of dignity. This stands in contrast to heteronomous inclinations (desires, emotions).

3. **Duty:** Moral action flows from duty (recognition of the Categorical Imperative), not from inclination, consequence, or emotion. An action has moral worth only if done from duty, not merely in accordance with duty.

4. **Good Will:** The only unconditional good is the good will — the intention to act rightly from duty, independent of outcomes or desires. Kant opens the *Grounding* with this: "Nothing in the world ... can be regarded as good without limitation except a good will."

5. **Moral Law & Reason:** Morality is grounded in reason, not in nature, sentiment, or God's command (heteronomy). Rational agents *create* the moral law through the structure of their rationality.

### Epistemology & Metaphysics

- **Synthesis:** Kant famously synthesized rationalism (Descartes, Leibniz) with empiricism (Hume). Knowledge requires both concepts (from reason) and intuitions (from sensory experience).
- **The Categorical-Empirical Distinction:** Some knowledge (mathematics, logic, metaphysical principles) is *a priori* (prior to experience) and universal; other knowledge is *a posteriori* (from experience) and particular.
- **Phenomena & Noumena:** We know things as they appear to us (phenomena, shaped by our cognitive structures of space and time); we cannot know things-in-themselves (noumena).

**Primary Text:** Immanuel Kant, *Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten* (*Grounding for the Metaphysics of Morals*), 1785. A foundational short treatise on Kantian ethics.

**Argument trajectory:**
- **Section I:** Kant derives the Categorical Imperative from common-sense morality. He argues that ordinary moral thinking presupposes the CI, even if people don't articulate it explicitly. (Common-sense and the CI are continuous, not opposed.)
- **Section II:** Kant argues that rational beings *must* be subject to the CI (autonomy + rational agency). Includes the famous formulation: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
- **Section III:** Kant argues that freedom and causality can be reconciled; moral agents are free insofar as they are rational. (Resolves the third antinomy of reason.)
