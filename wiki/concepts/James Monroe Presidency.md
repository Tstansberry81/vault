# James Monroe Presidency (1817–1825)

The fifth U.S. presidency, spanning two terms. Monroe's tenure — often called the **Era of Good Feelings** — was defined by the collapse of organized party opposition, explosive territorial expansion, and the first serious sectional crisis over slavery. Three events dominate the APUSH treatment: the **[[Monroe Doctrine]]**, the **annexation of Florida** via the [[Adams-Onís Treaty]], and the **[[Missouri Compromise]]**.

## Era of Good Feelings (1817–1825)
- The Federalist Party collapsed after the Hartford Convention's (1814) perceived treason during the War of 1812, leaving the Democratic-Republicans as the only organized national party.
- Monroe ran essentially **unopposed** in 1820 (one Electoral College dissenter). The era of partisan warfare that defined Adams and Jefferson gave way to surface unity.
- Beneath the surface: **sectionalism** (slavery, tariffs, internal improvements) was already fracturing the coalition. The Era of Good Feelings was partly illusion — the Missouri crisis (1820) made that unmistakable.

## The three pillars

### 1. Annexation of Florida — [[Adams-Onís Treaty]] (1819)
- Monroe dispatched **Andrew Jackson** to suppress Seminole raids on the Florida border. Jackson exceeded his orders, seizing Spanish forts and executing two British subjects.
- Monroe and Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** exploited the *fait accompli* diplomatically. Adams negotiated Spain's cession of all Florida to the U.S., with the U.S. paying $5 million in citizens' claims and renouncing claims to Texas.
- Crucially, Spain also ceded Pacific Northwest claims — making it the *Transcontinental* Treaty and extending U.S. sovereignty coast-to-coast for the first time.
- Monroe's strategic vision: natural defensive boundaries on all sides (Great Lakes, Atlantic, Gulf, Rockies/Pacific).

### 2. Missouri Compromise — [[Missouri Compromise]] (1820)
- Missouri's application for statehood as a slave state triggered the first great sectional crisis.
- Compromise: Missouri (slave) + Maine (free) admitted simultaneously; slavery banned north of **36°30′** in all remaining Louisiana Purchase territory.
- Monroe signed but did not drive the deal — **Henry Clay** brokered it in Congress.
- Jefferson: *"a fire bell in the night"* — correctly reading it as the opening salvo of what would become the Civil War.

### 3. Monroe Doctrine — [[Monroe Doctrine]] (1823)
- Embedded in Monroe's December 1823 message to Congress; written substantially by **John Quincy Adams**.
- Declared the Western Hemisphere closed to new European colonization or intervention.
- In practice: unenforceable without the Royal Navy — the boldness of the proclamation rested on British sea power.
- Followed naturally from the Adams-Onís Treaty: territorial consolidation (1819) → ideological declaration (1823).

## Cabinet: JQA as the architect
Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** was the intellectual engine of Monroe's foreign policy. Adams negotiated Florida, refused the British joint declaration on Latin America, and authored the core of the Doctrine. Monroe provided executive authority and strategic instinct; Adams provided the diplomatic precision. Their partnership is the paradigm case of a great Secretary of State shaping a presidency.

## Historical assessment
Monroe's presidency solidified the U.S. as a continental power and articulated a foreign-policy doctrine that would last two centuries — but it also exposed the fault line that 40 years later would produce the Civil War. The Era of Good Feelings masked, rather than resolved, the structural tension between slave and free states.
