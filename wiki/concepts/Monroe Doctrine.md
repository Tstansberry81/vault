# Monroe Doctrine (1823)

A statement of U.S. foreign policy principles embedded in President James Monroe's December 1823 annual message to Congress. Authored substantially by Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams**, it defined the Western Hemisphere as a zone closed to new European colonization and intervention — with sweeping long-term consequences for American foreign policy.

## Context: three converging threats (1821–1823)

1. **Russia's Pacific claims:** Czar Alexander I's 1821 *ukaz* (edict) asserted Russian sovereignty over a stretch of the Pacific Northwest and banned foreign vessels within 100 miles of the coastline.
2. **The Holy Alliance:** France, Austria, Russia, and Prussia federated under a reactionary anti-constitutional banner. The Troppau Principle (1820) authorized intervention in states where revolutions threatened the established order.
3. **France in Spain:** In April 1823 France invaded Spain to suppress constitutionalists and restore King Ferdinand VII — raising the specter that Spain's American colonies would be next.

## The British offer and Adams's refusal
British Foreign Minister **George Canning** approached American minister to London **Richard Rush** with a proposal for a *joint* Anglo-American declaration warning the Holy Allies away from Spanish America. Secretary Adams rejected joint action. His reasoning: Canning refused to commit to Spanish American *independence*, which Adams feared signaled future British duplicity — leaving the U.S. exposed if Britain reversed course. Adams preferred the U.S. to act unilaterally rather than appear to "come in as a cock-boat in the wake of the British man-of-war."

## What Adams actually wrote
- **Noncolonization paragraph** (sole Adams authorship): declared the Western Hemisphere closed to new European colonization — which Adams himself later called "a warning to Great Britain herself," not just the continental monarchies.
- **Two nonintervention paragraphs:** Monroe and the cabinet shaped these; Adams "deeply influenced" them.

## The Doctrine's internal tension
Jay Sexton emphasizes that the 1823 message expressed *both* American confidence and American anxiety simultaneously. The Monroe cabinet genuinely believed republican ideals would "transform global politics," yet they were also acutely aware that U.S. military power could not actually enforce the Doctrine without the Royal Navy standing behind it. It was a bold proclamation resting on British sea power — a paradox the framers understood but did not advertise.

## Later invocations and distortions

| Era | Use |
|-----|-----|
| **Civil War (1861–65)** | Lincoln and Seward *avoided* explicit invocation while France occupied Mexico — fearing it would further antagonize Europe at a moment when the Union needed neutrality. Seward instead dropped hints to London, hoping British pressure would push France out. |
| **Late 19th century** | Reinterpreted under Roosevelt Corollary (1904) as license for U.S. intervention in Latin America. |
| **20th century** | Recast as evidence of rising American power; the acquiescence of Old World powers to the Doctrine treated as proof of U.S. authority. |

## Relationship to the Adams-Onís Treaty
The Doctrine followed four years after the [[Adams-Onís Treaty]] (1819), which removed Spain as a direct territorial rival and extended U.S. claims to the Pacific. The two moves together — territorial consolidation then ideological declaration — form Monroe's signature foreign-policy sequence.
