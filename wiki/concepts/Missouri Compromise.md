# Missouri Compromise (1820)

A landmark congressional settlement that temporarily resolved the first major sectional crisis over slavery's westward expansion. Brokered primarily by **Henry Clay**, it defined the terms under which new slave and free states would enter the Union.

## Background: the crisis
Missouri's 1819 application for statehood as a *slave state* detonated the latent sectional conflict the Constitution had papered over. New York congressman **James Tallmadge Jr.** introduced an amendment to Missouri's enabling act that would ban new slaves from entering Missouri and gradually emancipate those already there. The House passed it; the Senate blocked it. The nation split along geographic lines for the first time in a way that looked existential.

The arithmetic problem: in 1819 there were **11 free states and 11 slave states**, giving the South equal Senate representation. Missouri's admission as a slave state would tip that balance — and set a precedent for the territories beyond.

## The settlement (three parts)
1. **Missouri admitted as a slave state.**
2. **Maine admitted as a free state** (split from Massachusetts) — preserving the 11/11 parity in the Senate.
3. **The 36°30′ line:** In all territory of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′ (Missouri's southern border), slavery was *forever prohibited*. Territory south of the line was open to slavery.

## Monroe's role
President Monroe signed the Compromise on March 6, 1820. His administration was largely passive — Clay and congressional leaders drove the deal — but Monroe's Era of Good Feelings atmosphere had suppressed party conflict enough that a cross-sectional compromise was still possible. Monroe privately worried the crisis signaled a structural threat to the Union; Jefferson famously called it "a fire bell in the night."

## Why it mattered — and why it only deferred the crisis
- It settled the immediate dispute but enshrined the principle that Congress *could* legislate on slavery in the territories — a claim the South would later bitterly contest.
- The 36°30′ line held for three decades before the [[Kansas-Nebraska Act]] (1854) repealed it, reigniting sectional war.
- The Missouri crisis previewed the political logic that eventually broke the Democratic-Republican Party and ended the Era of Good Feelings.

## APUSH frame
The Missouri Compromise is one of the most-tested events on the APUSH exam. Key facts to know:
- Year: **1820** (signed March 6)
- Architect: **Henry Clay** ("the Great Compromiser")
- Maine / Missouri paired entry
- **36°30′** as the dividing line
- Precursor to the Compromise of 1850 and the Kansas-Nebraska Act
