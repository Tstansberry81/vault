# Adams-Onís Treaty (Transcontinental Treaty, 1819)

Also called the **Transcontinental Treaty** or **Florida Purchase Treaty**. A pivotal diplomatic agreement between Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** and Spanish minister **Luis de Onís**, ratified in 1821.

## Key terms
- Spain ceded **East and West Florida** to the United States and renounced all territorial claims there.
- The U.S. renounced all claims to **Texas**.
- The U.S. agreed to pay **$5 million** to settle American citizens' claims against Spain.
- Crucially: Spain ceded all claims to the **Pacific Northwest**, extending nominal U.S. sovereignty to the Pacific Ocean — hence "Transcontinental."
- The treaty defined the **western limits of the Louisiana Territory** via the Sabine and Arkansas Rivers.

## Monroe's strategic logic
President Monroe had dispatched Andrew Jackson to the Florida borderlands nominally to suppress Seminole raids. Jackson's aggressive campaign effectively seized Spanish-held forts, giving the U.S. a decisive bargaining position. Historian Harlow Giles Unger argues Monroe had thus "seized almost the entire continent" before the treaty was even signed.

Monroe prized boundaries with **natural defensive advantages**: Great Lakes / St. Lawrence (north), Atlantic Ocean (east), Gulf of Mexico (south), Sabine/Arkansas Rivers (southwest), Rocky Mountains and Pacific (west). The treaty delivered most of these.

## Significance in the APUSH frame
- Together with the [[Louisiana Purchase]] (1803), the Adams-Onís Treaty transformed the early republic into a continental power.
- It set the stage for the [[Monroe Doctrine]] (1823) by removing Spain as a direct territorial rival and extending U.S. claims coast-to-coast.
- Adams's diplomatic skill here is distinct from Monroe's executive audacity — a recurring theme in early American foreign policy: civilian diplomacy riding behind military *fait accompli*.
