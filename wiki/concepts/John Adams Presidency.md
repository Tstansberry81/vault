# John Adams Presidency (1797–1801)

Adams's single term, marked by the undeclared **Quasi-War** with France, the deeply controversial **Alien and Sedition Acts**, internal Federalist fractures driven by Alexander Hamilton, and a peace settlement that likely cost Adams the 1800 election.

## The Quasi-War with France (1798–1800)
Adams inherited deteriorating Franco-American relations from Washington. France, offended by the Jay Treaty (1794) and treating American ships as hostile, was seizing U.S. vessels in the Caribbean.

- Adams called the conflict the **"Half-War"**; historians later named it the **Quasi-War**.
- He sent envoys (Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, John Marshall, Elbridge Gerry) to Paris; they were kept waiting, then subjected to the **XYZ Affair**.

### XYZ Affair
Three secret agents of Foreign Minister **Talleyrand** (labeled X, Y, Z in dispatches) approached the American envoys:
- Demanded a personal bribe of **$250,000** for Talleyrand.
- Required a loan of **$10,000,000** to the French Republic — framed as compensation for Adams's "insulting" May 1797 congressional speech.
- Adams released the (initially mis-sent undecoded) dispatches to Congress on **March 5**. Public outrage followed, rallying the country behind Adams temporarily.

Jefferson's earlier characterization — that America was being run by "timid men who prefer the calm of despotism to the boisterous sea of liberty" — looked misplaced in the context of Adams's hawkish response.

## Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)

Four laws passed by the Federalist-controlled Congress:

| Act | Substance |
|-----|-----------|
| **Naturalization Act** | Raised the required residency for citizenship from **5 to 14 years** |
| **Alien Friends Act** | Authorized the president to deport any alien he considered "dangerous" |
| **Alien Enemies Act** | Authorized deportation of aliens from enemy nations during wartime |
| **Sedition Act** | Criminalized "seditious libel" against the government or president |

### Enforcement
- The Republican press was targeted *before* the Sedition Act even cleared Congress: **Ben Bache** of *The Aurora* was arrested in June for libeling the president (died before trial).
- **Matthew Lyon** (Republican congressman, Vermont) convicted of seditious libel for an article in the *Vermont Journal*; fined $1,000 and imprisoned four months in an unheated former slave cell — while simultaneously winning re-election.
- **John Daly Burk** (Irish alien, editor of *New York Time Piece*): arrested; Aaron Burr paid bail; Burk fled to Virginia. Many French aliens simply left the country.

### Adams's actual behavior vs. Federalist intent
- Adams **never invoked the Alien Act** to deport anyone — despite Secretary of State **Timothy Pickering** pushing for mass deportations.
- This restraint is historically significant: the acts were more political weapon than administrative program under Adams himself.

## Convention of 1800 (Treaty of Môrtefontaine)
The peace agreement ending the Quasi-War:
- Established peace between the U.S. and France.
- Recognized U.S. rights to trade (in non-contraband) with belligerents.
- **Did not** provide indemnification for American property seized by France — deferred for future negotiation.
- **Did not** resolve the status of the Franco-American alliance (1778) — also deferred.
- Adams considered it less than he had hoped for; it was politically costly, splitting Federalists and handing Jefferson the 1800 election.

## The Hamilton problem
Hamilton and his faction within the Federalist Party wanted Adams to push harder toward open war with France — partly to justify the expensive standing army Hamilton controlled. Hamilton's intrigues with Adams's own cabinet (Pickering, McHenry) undermined Adams throughout his term. Adams eventually purged the Hamilton loyalists, but too late to save his presidency.

## Historical significance (APUSH frame)
Adams's presidency demonstrates the tension between executive restraint and party-line pressure in the early republic. His willingness to seek peace with France at political cost to himself is one of the more under-appreciated acts of statesmanship in the founding era. The Sedition Act, conversely, remains one of the most significant early challenges to First Amendment freedoms — a precedent that haunted American civil liberties law for generations.
